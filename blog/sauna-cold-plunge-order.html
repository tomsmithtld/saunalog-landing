Sauna vs Cold Plunge: Which Should You Do First?

By Tom | February 2026 | 5 min read

if you've been to a spa, a gym with both, or a Scandinavian bathhouse, you've probably wondered: hot first or cold first? the answer depends on what you're trying to get out of it.

The short answer

End on hot (sauna last)

Better for relaxation, sleep, muscle recovery, and winding down. Leaves you warm and calm.

End on cold (plunge last)

Better for energy, dopamine, alertness, and metabolic benefits. Leaves you buzzing and sharp.

Huberman specifically recommends ending on cold if you want the dopamine and norepinephrine benefits. ending on hot negates some of the cold exposure response because the warming undoes the "let your body rewarm itself" mechanism.

Contrast therapy: the Finnish way

traditional Finnish sauna culture involves alternating between hot and cold. sauna for 10-15 minutes, then a cold lake or snow roll, then back to the sauna. multiple rounds.

this is contrast therapy, and it has its own set of benefits:

  • vascular conditioning. the rapid switching between vasodilation (heat) and vasoconstriction (cold) is like a workout for your blood vessels. improved vascular function is one of the most consistent findings in contrast therapy research.
  • reduced inflammation. alternating temperatures helps flush inflammatory markers from tissues. this is why athletes use contrast baths after training.
  • mood and energy. the combination produces a unique mental state. alert but not anxious, relaxed but not sleepy. it's different from either hot or cold alone.

What the research says about order

there's surprisingly little research specifically comparing "hot first" vs "cold first" for the same person doing contrast therapy. most studies look at either sauna benefits or cold immersion benefits separately.

what we do know:

  • ending on cold preserves the cold exposure dopamine response. Huberman cites this consistently. if you want the mood and alertness benefits of cold exposure, don't warm up afterwards.
  • ending on hot is better for sleep. the temperature drop after a sauna (as your body cools down) is a strong sleep signal. ending on cold gives you a temperature rise (as your body warms back up), which can interfere with sleep onset.
  • for cardiovascular benefits, the order probably doesn't matter much. both hot and cold exposure independently improve cardiovascular markers, and contrast therapy adds vascular flexibility training regardless of order.

my routine: morning sessions end on cold (I want the energy). evening sessions end on hot (I want the sleep benefit). it's a simple rule that works well.

A practical contrast protocol

if you have access to both a sauna and cold water, here's a simple protocol:

  • round 1: sauna 10-15 minutes, then cold (1-3 minutes)
  • round 2: sauna 10-15 minutes, then cold (1-3 minutes)
  • round 3: sauna 10-15 minutes, then cold (1-3 minutes) OR sauna only (depending on morning/evening)

total time: 45-60 minutes. this gives you roughly 30-45 minutes of sauna (counts toward your weekly 57-minute target) plus 3-9 minutes of cold (counts toward your 11-minute weekly cold target if you follow Huberman's protocol for both).

What if you only have one?

both sauna and cold exposure have independent, well-studied benefits. you don't need both to get value.

  • sauna only: focus on hitting 57 minutes per week. the cardiovascular and longevity benefits are well established. end your sauna sessions with a cold shower for a mini contrast effect.
  • cold only: focus on 11 minutes per week (Huberman protocol). the dopamine and norepinephrine benefits don't require heat first. a cold shower at the end of your regular shower is enough.

safety: going from extreme heat to extreme cold (or vice versa) puts stress on your cardiovascular system. don't do contrast therapy if you have heart issues without talking to your doctor. never go from sauna directly into very cold water without some transition time. and never do either alone in open water.
