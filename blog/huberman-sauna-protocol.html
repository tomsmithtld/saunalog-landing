Huberman Sauna Protocol: 57 Minutes Per Week

By Tom | February 2026 | 6 min read

Andrew Huberman cites a specific sauna protocol on his podcast that he says is associated with significant health benefits, particularly cardiovascular health and longevity. the number he references is 57 minutes per week.

this comes from Finnish sauna research, some of the largest and longest-running studies on heat exposure and health outcomes. here's the protocol and the evidence behind it.

The core protocol

Huberman Sauna Protocol

1
57 minutes total per week. Split across 2-7 sessions. Huberman suggests at least 2-3 sessions per week. More frequent is fine.
2
Temperature: 80-100C (176-212F). This is traditional Finnish sauna territory. Hot enough to be uncomfortable but safe for healthy adults.
3
Dry sauna preferred. The research primarily studied dry saunas (low humidity). Infrared and steam rooms may have benefits, but the evidence is strongest for traditional dry heat.
4
Hydrate before and after. You lose a significant amount of water through sweat. Drink 500ml before and at least 500ml after each session.

The Finnish research

the key study Huberman references is the Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease (KIHD) study, a long-running Finnish study that followed over 2,300 men for 20+ years.

the headline findings are striking:

40%
Lower all-cause mortality (4-7 sessions/week vs 1)
50%
Lower cardiovascular death risk
65%
Lower Alzheimer's risk

these are observational results (Finnish men who sauna a lot might have other healthy habits), but the dose-response relationship is strong. more sauna sessions per week correlated with better outcomes, even after controlling for exercise, diet, and socioeconomic factors.

Why 57 minutes?

Huberman pulls this from the research showing that the benefits scale with total weekly exposure time. the studies showed the biggest health improvements at roughly an hour per week of sauna use.

a practical split might be:

  • 3 sessions x 19 minutes: classic. three visits to the gym or sauna club per week.
  • 4 sessions x 14 minutes: shorter but more frequent. easier to fit into a routine.
  • 2 sessions x 28 minutes: fewer visits but longer sessions. works if you can only make it to a sauna twice a week.

the research doesn't suggest that longer individual sessions are better than shorter ones, as long as the weekly total is in the right range.

What actually happens in the heat

sauna exposure triggers a cascade of physiological responses:

  • heat shock proteins. your body produces proteins that help repair damaged cells and prevent protein misfolding (associated with Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases).
  • cardiovascular conditioning. your heart rate increases to 100-150 bpm. sauna mimics mild to moderate cardiovascular exercise. blood vessels dilate, blood pressure drops afterwards.
  • growth hormone. some studies show significant increases in growth hormone following sauna sessions, particularly when combined with fasting or exercise.
  • endorphins. the "sauna high" is real. endorphin release during heat exposure creates a genuine mood lift that lasts for hours.

Huberman has said on his podcast that sauna is one of the highest-return health practices because it mimics exercise at a cardiovascular level while being accessible to people who might not be able to exercise (injury, disability, etc.). the data supports this, but it's not a replacement for actual exercise.

Sauna and sleep

sauna in the evening can significantly improve sleep quality. the mechanism is your core body temperature: after a sauna, your temperature drops below baseline as your body overcorrects. this temperature drop is one of the strongest signals for sleep onset.

timing: finish your sauna 1-2 hours before bed. this gives your body time to cool down. a sauna immediately before bed might keep you too warm to fall asleep easily.

Practical tips for hitting 57 minutes

  • track it. it's surprisingly easy to lose count. I'd think I was doing 3 sessions a week, but when I tracked it, it was more like 1.5. SaunaLog was built for exactly this.
  • combine with gym visits. most gyms have a sauna. 15 minutes after your workout is an easy way to get your sessions in.
  • start lower if you're new. 10 minutes at 80C is plenty for beginners. build up over weeks. there's no benefit to suffering through 20 minutes if you're not used to it.
  • bring water in with you. not all saunas allow this, but if yours does, sip throughout. dehydration is the main risk with regular sauna use.

safety: don't use a sauna if you've been drinking alcohol. avoid it if you have low blood pressure, are pregnant, or have a heart condition without talking to your doctor first. leave immediately if you feel dizzy, nauseous, or faint. and always hydrate.
