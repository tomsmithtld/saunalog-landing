Infrared Sauna vs Traditional Sauna: What's the Difference?

By Tom | February 2026 | 6 min read

every gym and wellness centre seems to have an infrared sauna now. they're marketed as a gentler, more effective alternative to traditional saunas. but are they actually better? or just different?

the honest answer: they do different things, and the "better" one depends on what you're looking for.

How they actually work

a traditional (Finnish) sauna heats the air to 80-100C using a stove (electric or wood-fired). rocks get hot, you throw water on them for steam. the hot air heats your body from the outside in. you sweat because the air around you is extremely hot.

an infrared sauna uses infrared panels (usually far-infrared) that emit light in the infrared spectrum. this light penetrates your skin directly and heats your body from the inside out. the air temperature stays much lower, typically 45-65C.

the key difference: in a traditional sauna, you're heating the room and your body heats as a side effect. in an infrared sauna, you're heating your body directly and the room stays relatively cool.

What the research says about each

the big Finnish studies on sauna health benefits (the ones showing 40% lower all-cause mortality with 4-7 sessions per week) all used traditional Finnish saunas. this is important because it means the strongest longevity evidence is for traditional saunas specifically.

infrared saunas have their own research, but it's more limited. studies show benefits for chronic pain, blood pressure, and cardiovascular function. a 2009 study in the Canadian Journal of Diabetes found that infrared sauna use improved quality of life and lowered blood pressure in type 2 diabetes patients.

the problem with comparing them directly is that most studies only test one type. there are very few head-to-head trials. so when someone says "infrared is better," they're usually comparing infrared studies against traditional sauna studies, which isn't the same as a direct comparison.

Cardiovascular benefits

both types raise your heart rate and dilate blood vessels. a traditional sauna session can push your heart rate to 100-150 bpm, similar to moderate exercise. infrared saunas produce a similar but gentler cardiovascular response because the air temperature is lower.

the Finnish studies (traditional sauna) showed significant reductions in cardiovascular disease risk. infrared sauna studies show improvements in blood pressure and endothelial function. both seem to benefit your heart, through slightly different mechanisms.

Detoxification claims

this is where the marketing gets ahead of the science. infrared sauna companies often claim their saunas are better for "detoxification" because the infrared light penetrates deeper, causing a more "detoxifying" sweat.

the reality: sweat is mostly water, sodium, and trace minerals. both sauna types make you sweat. there's some evidence that sweat contains small amounts of heavy metals and BPA, but the amounts are tiny compared to what your liver and kidneys process daily.

if you want to detoxify, your liver is already doing that. saunas of either type aren't a meaningful detox pathway. they're good for other things. just not that.

The experience

this matters more than most guides admit. the best sauna is the one you'll actually use consistently.

  • traditional sauna: intensely hot air, steam optional. you feel the heat immediately and it's oppressive. sessions typically 15-20 minutes. many people find it meditative. the heat forces you to be present because you can't really ignore it.
  • infrared sauna: warm but breathable air. takes longer to start sweating (often 10-15 min before you feel much). sessions typically 30-45 minutes. more comfortable overall. easier to read, listen to podcasts, or just relax.

some people love the intensity of a traditional sauna and find infrared boring. others find traditional saunas unbearable and prefer the gentle warmth of infrared. this is personal preference, not a quality difference.

Cost and practicality

if you're buying for home use:

  • infrared: portable units start around 500-1000 quid. plug into a standard socket. no special installation. some fold up for storage.
  • traditional: a proper barrel sauna or indoor sauna starts around 2000-5000 quid installed. needs proper ventilation, sometimes a dedicated electrical circuit, and a spot in your garden or bathroom.

for gym or spa use, the cost is irrelevant. use whatever they have. both are better than neither.

Which should you choose?

I use a traditional sauna because my gym has one and the Finnish research convinced me. but I wouldn't turn down an infrared session.

here's the practical guidance:

  • go traditional if: you want the strongest evidence base, you enjoy intense heat, you have access to one at a gym or can invest in a home unit.
  • go infrared if: you find traditional saunas too intense, you want something for home use without major installation, you have chronic pain (the infrared pain research is promising), or you prefer longer, more comfortable sessions.
  • either way: consistency matters more than type. 3-4 sessions per week of either type will give you health benefits. doing one session of the "perfect" sauna type once a month won't do much.

same safety rules for both: stay hydrated (drink water before, during, and after). don't use the sauna after heavy drinking. if you feel dizzy or unwell, get out. limit sessions to 20 minutes for traditional, 45 minutes for infrared. talk to your GP if you have heart conditions, low blood pressure, or are pregnant.
